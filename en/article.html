Origins of money

Before Bitcoin, before gold, before barter in your history books, humans everywhere and in every era invented some form of money. Why?

The question sounds silly. Money is so familiar we forget it's an invention. But think: what was there before? Direct trade. I have fish, you have bread, we make a deal. Simple.

Simple, and almost never works.

For a direct trade to happen, I have to want what you have, at the same moment you want what I have. Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. It's a brutal coordination problem. If you want fish but I don't want your bread, the trade dies, even if we both had something to gain.

The fix: a middle commodity

At some point, someone saw the shortcut. Instead of looking for the perfect counterparty, I accept a middle commodity, something almost everyone accepts. Salt. Shells. Cattle. Pieces of metal. I take that commodity for my fish, and later use it to get what I actually want.

That middle commodity is money. And the important point is that it wasn't decreed. No one sat down and said "starting today, this is money." It emerged from use, gradually, because it solved the double-coincidence problem.

The economist Carl Menger formalized this in 1892. Money, to Menger, is a spontaneous institution, a byproduct of people trying to trade more efficiently.

What makes a good money-commodity

If money emerges from people's choices, which commodity wins? A few properties matter:

  • Durability. Apples rot. Gold doesn't.
  • Divisibility. You need to pay in different sizes. Cattle are terrible at this.
  • Portability. You need to carry it. Giant stones (yes, that existed) don't work well.
  • Scarcity. If anyone can produce more, value disappears. Shells worked until someone showed up with a boat full of them.
  • Verifiability. You need to know it's what it looks like. Precious metals can be weighed and tested.

For millennia, gold won this competition. By survival, not by decree.

Why this matters for Bitcoin

The Bitcoin argument starts here. If money is a technology that emerges from people's choices, and if there are objective properties that make one money-commodity better than another, then the natural question is: is there any commodity with those properties, but digital?

For decades, the answer was no. Bits can be copied infinitely. Digital scarcity was a paradox. Verifiability without a central authority was impossible. That's exactly the wall Bitcoin walked through in 2009.

But before we get there, we need to understand what happened to money after gold. Why gold lost. Why governments wanted it to lose. And why that matters.

That's the next station.
